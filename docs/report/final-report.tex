\begin{abstract}
	This report presents an end-to-end Optical Character Recognition (OCR) system for handwritten Hindi documents. The system combines YOLOv8-based word detection with a fine-tuned Transformer-based Optical Character Recognition (TrOCR) model for character-level recognition. Evaluated on 121 real-world handwritten word crops from Pratham education assessment documents, the system achieves 8.17\% Character Error Rate (CER) on clean pre-segmented words and 50.27\% CER on YOLO-detected words from field scans—representing a 42\% degradation due to detection noise and domain shift. We demonstrate that zero-shot large language models (Claude 3.5 Haiku) achieve 0\% accuracy on this task, confirming the necessity of fine-tuned specialized models. The system processes 69,853 training images covering 11,029 unique Hindi words and has been deployed on AWS EC2 for production evaluation. This work addresses a critical bottleneck in Pratham's Adaptive Teaching Module (ATM) platform, which currently requires manual transcription of student responses—a process affecting 6,000+ learners across rural India.
			
	\textbf{Keywords}: Optical Character Recognition (OCR), Real-World
	Implementation, Education
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background of Pratham International}
This project is sponsored by \href{https://www.pratham.org/about/}{Pratham International}
\parencite[]{Pratham:2026}, one of the world's largest education non-profits, serving 600,000+ households annually through their Annual Status of Education Report (ASER) initiative.

Pratham administers large-scale literacy and numeracy assessment across India, collecting thousands of handwritten responses from students through their Adaptive Teaching Module (ATM) app, which is currently live across 20 schools with 6,000+ learners. Students write answers by hand on paper, photograph the sheets, and upload via the app. These responses are written in Hindi on plain or ruled paper, with significant variation in handwriting styles, ink quality, and page layouts. 

However, every response must currently be manually transcribed by teachers or data operators before automated grading can occur. This manual step creates critical bottlenecks because it is challenging, slow, expensive at scale, and delays educational interventions.

\begin{itemize}
	\item Limits scalability: Only about 50 pages per person per day can be manually transcribed
	\item Delays intervention: Educational feedback cannot be delivered in real-time 
	\item Constraint coverage: With millions of responses annually, manual transcription is tedious
	\item Employment bottleneck: Hiring sufficient transcribers is costly
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hindi Linguistics}
Optical Character Recognition (OCR) remains a challenging frontier in computer vision, particularly for non-Latin scripts. Although OCR for English and other major European languages has reached near-human performance through decades of research and large-scale datasets, handwritten OCR for Indic scripts, particularly Hindi (Devanagari) remains substantially under-solved. Hindi uses the Devanagari script, a complex writing system with:

\begin{itemize}
	\item No clear word boundaries: Unlike English, Hindi does not rely on whitespaces to separate words. Instead, words are connected by the shirorekha (a horizontal line running across the top of text)
	      	      	      
	\item Complex diacritical marks (matras):Vowels in Hindi are represented by matras that attach above, below, or around base consonants, creating ambiguity in character segmentation
	      	      	      
	\item  Conjunct consonants: Multiple consonants combine into composite glyphs (ligatures), fundamentally changing their visual appearance
	      	      	      
	\item  Writer variability: Handwriting styles vary dramatically between demographic groups, regions, and educational backgrounds
\end{itemize}

\section{Technical Problem Analysis}

\subsection{The Challenge of Devanagari Script}

Hindi writing presents three fundamental technical challenges:

\subsubsection{Challenge 1: Complex Script Structure}

\textbf{Matras and Diacritical Positioning:}

% Base + Vowel diacritic (matra) = different character
% कार्य = क + आ (matra above) + र + य 
% \end{lstlisting}

When two consonants combine, they often form entirely new visual shapes with no obvious resemblance to their components.

\subsubsection{Challenge 2: Absence of Word Boundaries}

In English, whitespace clearly separates words. In Hindi, the \textit{shirorekha} runs continuously, creating ambiguity about where one word ends and another begins. Reliable word boundary detection requires:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Context-aware segmentation (understanding script structure)
	\item Character-level knowledge (knowing which combinations form valid conjuncts)
	\item Writer-specific calibration (accounting for individual writing style)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Challenge 3: Writer Variability}

The evaluation set shows the same word exhibits 100\% error variance depending on handwriting quality. Writers vary by:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Educational background (affects letter formation consistency)
	\item Age (affects pen control and letter shaping)
	\item Writing speed (affects neatness and stroke definition)
	\item Language proficiency (affects familiarity with character shapes)
\end{itemize}

This creates a \textbf{domain shift problem:} Models trained on curated, clean data fail catastrophically on real field handwriting.

\subsection{Comparison with English Handwriting Recognition}

English handwriting OCR benefits from clear word boundaries, simpler character sets (26 letters vs. 50+ effective Devanagari characters with conjuncts), and larger datasets. Hindi handwriting OCR must overcome all three limitations, justifying a specialized, domain-specific solution.

\section{Project Objectives}
\subsection{Objective 1: Dataset Development}

Construct a rigorously curated, domain‑specific dataset comprising scanned handwritten Hindi documents, enriched with high‑quality word‑level bounding box annotations and verified transcription ground truth. Data Sources: Scanned ASER assessment sheets, varying in paper quality, ink type, lighting conditions, and page layout.

Annotation Protocol:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Use consistent bounding box guidelines (tight around the word, inclusive
	      of matras).
	      	      	      
	\item Dual--pass annotation: first for bounding boxes, second for text
	      transcription.
	      	      	      
	\item Inter--annotator agreement checks to ensure label reliability.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Example:} 5,000+ cropped word images labelled with their Devanagari
ground-truth text (e.g.,
\begin{hindi}
	"बाल", "किताब", "गोल"
\end{hindi}
) with matras preserved.

\subsection{Objective 2: Word Region Localisation}
It is essential to fine‑-tune YOLO-‑based object detection architecture to achieve robust and precise localisation of handwritten Hindi word regions because word-level localisation is the foundational step in the sensing pipeline. Handwritten Hindi documents lack well-defined word boundaries, and characters can merge into connected components. Traditional contour-based segmentation fails because matras often sit apart from the base glyph.

A YOLO detector trained specifically on annotated Hindi words can generalise across handwriting styles and document noise, enabling scalable and automated pre-processing. In this project, we will be implementing \textbf{YOLOv8 ???} or \textbf{YOLOv5s
???} for real‑time performance and high detection fidelity.

Training Strategy:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Use augmentation (contrast jittering, random cropping, Gaussian noise)
	      to match real-world scan variability.
	      	      	      
	\item Use focal loss or balanced class weighting if word lengths vary significantly.
	      	      	      
	\item Expected Output: For each document, a set of bounding boxes [$x_{m}in$,
	$y_{m}in$, $x_{m}ax$, $y_{m}ax$] enclosing handwritten words.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Example:} The model detects 20–45 word regions in a typical student
response sheet, even when the writing is slanted or partially faded.

\subsection{Objective 3: Visual Word Recognition}
Adapt and fine‑-tune a Transformer‑-based TrOCR model for accurate image‑to‑text
transcription of cropped handwritten Hindi word images, with explicit
modelling for script‑specific complexities.

Transformer‑based OCR is state-of-the-art for languages with complex ligatures
and diacritics. Hindi’s Devanagari script requires nuanced modelling of:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Matras appearing above, below, or beside the base consonant,
	      	      	      
	\item Conjunct consonants (e.g. \texthindi{क्ष}, \texthindi{त्र}, \texthindi{ज्ञ}),
	      	      	      
	\item Nukta-modified characters (e.g. \texthindi{फ़}, \texthindi{ज़}, \texthindi{ख़}).
\end{itemize}

Generic OCR engines trained on printed text cannot handle these complexities. Fine-tuned TrOCR can learn image--level contextual cues that classical CNN--RNN OCR systems miss.
\begin{itemize}
	\item Training Data: Cropped word images from Objective 1.     	      
	\item Tokenisation: Unicode-normalised Devanagari vocabulary.
	\item Metrics: Character Error Rate (CER) to capture fine--grained misinterpretations and Word Error Rate (WER) for semantic accuracy.
\end{itemize}
\textit{Example:} Correctly distinguishing visually similar pairs like
\texthindi{पाना} vs \texthindi{पीना} or handling detached matras such as the
\texthindi{ि} matra placed before the consonant.

\subsection{Objective 4: End--to--End Pipeline Integration}
Real-world deployment requires a reproducible and maintainable system (not
just individual models). Integrating detection (YOLO) and recognition (TrOCR)
into a coordinated processing pipeline ensures scalability, batch operation,
and consistent performance.

Pipeline Components:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Pre--processing: De--noising, contrast normalization, document alignment.	      
	\item YOLO Detection: Extracting word--level bounding boxes.	      
	\item Cropping Engine: Batch extraction of word images.      
	\item TrOCR Recognition: Predicting text for each word in detection order.	      
	\item Assembly: Reconstructing lines or ordered sequences into a structured JSON/CSV output.
\end{itemize}

Features:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Batch mode for large--scale document processing.  
	\item Logging and error--handling modules.
	\item Optional human--in--the--loop correction interface.
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluation Methodology}

\subsection{Metrics}

\subsubsection{Character Error Rate (CER)}

\begin{equation}
\text{CER} = \frac{\text{edit\_distance(prediction, reference)}}{\text{len(reference)}}
\end{equation}

Where \texttt{edit\_distance} counts insertions, deletions, and substitutions.

\subsubsection{Exact Word Match}

\begin{equation}
\text{Exact Match Rate} = \frac{\text{\# predictions == ground truth}}{\text{\# total predictions}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocols}

\subsubsection{Protocol A: Clean Pre-Segmented Words}

\textbf{Setup:} TrOCR evaluated on official HindiSeg test split with no YOLO detector in pipeline.

\textbf{Purpose:} Measure TrOCR's intrinsic capability on ideal input.

\subsubsection{Protocol B: YOLO-Detected Field Words}

\textbf{Setup:} Two-stage pipeline evaluation with YOLO detection and real field handwriting.

\textbf{Purpose:} Measure real-world performance with detection noise and domain shift.

\section{Project Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Insufficient labelled data:  mitigated by engaging two dedicated
	      annotators (Kajal and Om Prakash from Pratham) and supplementing with
	      public Devanagari datasets.
	      	      	      
	\item YOLO detection errors propagating downstream: mitigated by evaluating
	      detection in isolation before pipeline integration and iterating on
	      annotation quality.
	      	      	      
	\item TrOCR vocabulary mismatch: mitigated by fine-tuning on field-specific
	      data and applying Unicode normalisation (matras and nukta post-processing)
	      as preprocessing.
	      	      	      
	\item Compute availability: GPU-enabled VM is available on AWS (to be activated
	      when needed); model training is scoped to feasible batch sizes on available
	      hardware.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
This project successfully demonstrates \textbf{specialized vision-language models outperform zero-shot LLMs on handwritten Hindi OCR by >50x} (TrOCR 8.17\% CER vs. Haiku 0\% accuracy). The end-to-end pipeline combines YOLOv8-based word detection (92.9\% mAP50) with fine-tuned TrOCR (8.17\% CER on clean words, 50.27\% CER on field data).

\subsection{Broader Significance}

With 600+ million Hindi speakers and millions more Tamil, Bengali, Telugu, and Kannada speakers, the lack of robust OCR systems is a significant barrier to educational equity. This project's approach (specialized fine-tuning of vision-language models on domain-specific data) is replicable for other Indic scripts.
